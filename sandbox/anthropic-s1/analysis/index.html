Sandbox · Anthropic’s prospectus · Analysis

Does it read as if an AI wrote it?

Whether text carries a hidden mark that says an AI wrote it, and what counting words can and cannot tell you, tried on this filing and eight others.

A sandbox page, not part of the public record this site keeps. Everything below is Analysis: counts and comparisons, not a finding about who wrote the filing. This site is built with the help of Claude, made by Anthropic; Anthropic had no part in this page.

The short answer

No test can say who or what wrote this filing. On the word counts that research links to AI writing, it reads like an ordinary prospectus. On borrowed phrasing and vocabulary, it is less formulaic than most.

The filing says Claude wrote over 90 percent of the code added to Anthropic’s own codebase in September 2026. It says nothing we could find about how the prospectus itself was written. Whoever typed the words, the law puts the responsibility on people: under the Securities Act of 1933, the company, its directors, the officers who sign it and the underwriters can be held liable for false or misleading statements in it.

Is there a hidden mark?

Text carries no mark that everyone can read

  • No label comes with plain text. A web page like this filing has no field that records whether a person or a program wrote a sentence. This one names no software at all; it was submitted through a filing agent, and the agent’s number tells you who sent it, not who wrote it.
  • Some AI makers can hide a pattern in word choice. Google DeepMind’s SynthID Text, for example, nudges which words its models pick so that its own detector can later recognise the pattern. Only a detector built for that system can look for it, Google says it works less well on factual text, and its confidence drops when text is thoroughly rewritten or translated. No outsider can test a document against every AI maker’s private mark.
  • Invisible characters. It is often said that AI tools slip invisible characters into text. We counted zero-width spaces and joiners, byte-order marks, direction controls, Unicode tag characters, soft hyphens and variation selectors in all nine filings: there are 0 in all of them together.
  • The law asks AI makers, not readers. In the European Union, the AI Act now requires makers of AI systems that generate text, images, sound or video to mark their output in a machine-readable way that can be detected. The duty began on August 2, 2026, with extra time for systems already on sale. It does not give readers one mark to check, and it does not reach a U.S. filing written by people.
  • Detector programs are unreliable. OpenAI shut down its own detector in July 2023 for its low accuracy; on its own test it caught 26 percent of AI-written text and wrongly flagged 9 percent of human writing. A Stanford study found detectors often mistake writing by non-native English speakers for AI. We did not run one: it would mean sending the filing to another company for a verdict nobody could check.

What each column means

  • 21 study words: the words Juzek and Ward found AI models overuse (delve, showcasing, underscores, intricate, realm, groundbreaking and others), per 10,000 words.
  • 10 common markers: ordinary words whose use rose in research summaries after AI tools spread (across, additionally, comprehensive, crucial, within and others), per 10,000 words.
  • Em dashes: the long dash, often called an AI habit, per 1,000 words.
  • Sentence length: the average in words, and how much lengths vary. Writing by AI is often said to vary less.
  • Vocabulary variety: the share of different words in each 500-word stretch, averaged; higher means less repetition.
  • Shared phrasing: the share of the filing’s eight-word sequences that also appear in at least one of the other eight prospectuses (for the others: in at least one of the other seven, Anthropic’s left out). Higher means more standard wording.
What it shows

Reading the counts

  • The study words barely appear. Anthropic’s filing uses them 4 times in 221,030 words (0.18 per 10,000): within the range of the four filings written before ChatGPT (0.07 to 0.46) and below three of the four recent ones. Delve, showcasing, intricate and realm do not appear at all.
  • The common markers are higher (23.5 per 10,000, against 11.5 to 19.1 before ChatGPT and 15.0 to 19.0 recently), almost all of it two words, across (213 times) and within (186). Both are also ordinary legal and business words; the ownership table’s notes repeat the standard phrase about shares a holder can acquire within 60 days. So this count points both ways.
  • Em dashes are more frequent (1.31 per 1,000 words) than in the four older filings (0.49 to 1.10), and less frequent than in Circle’s of 2025 (1.98). Prospectuses use the dash to join section titles in cross-references, so this says little.
  • Sentences look like every other prospectus: 34.3 words on average with a spread of 20.0, against 31.7 to 34.6 and 19.0 to 21.6 in the other eight.
  • The vocabulary is the most varied of the nine (0.421, against 0.388 to 0.415 in the others).
  • It borrows the least standard wording. 13.5 percent of its eight-word sequences appear in another of these prospectuses, the lowest of the nine (the others: 13.6 to 34.7 percent). Much of it was written for this filing rather than carried over from earlier ones.

What this cannot tell you

Limits

  • Counts describe style, not authorship. People can write like a model, and edited AI output can read like a person.
  • Nine filings, all large technology companies, is a small set. The word lists come from studies of research summaries and peer reviews, not of legal documents.
  • A prospectus is written by many hands (company staff, two sets of lawyers, bankers and accountants) and reuses standard language, which flattens any one writer’s style.
  • Our reading of each filing rebuilds its text from the page layout; small differences in how lines are joined can move the sentence figures slightly.

Plain answers

Who pays for this site?

Nobody pays to appear on it. The site runs no advertising of its own and has no donors. It is published on GitHub's free public hosting.

Does this site track me?

This site counts nothing about its readers: no page sends a visit, a click or a place anywhere, except as described below. There is no account and no sign-in. Your reading settings and your companion are kept in this browser, on this device, and are never sent anywhere.

Where a page offers "Use my location", your place is worked out on your device and is not sent anywhere. Every page takes its type from this site. Three things come from other servers. The opening picture of Plain Congress shows the weather at the Capitol in Washington, from Open-Meteo; that request carries the Capitol's place, never yours. If you switch on street maps on a ballot or Forecasts page, OpenStreetMap's servers see which map squares are asked for, and the page says so. And if you type your address on a ballot or Forecasts page and press Look up, your browser sends it straight to the U.S. Census Bureau's address finder, which sends back where it is; this site never sees the address or keeps it.

What happens when I share a page?

A Share button sends a link. On a phone it opens your phone’s own share sheet; on a computer, a short list of places, such as e-mail, Facebook or LinkedIn, or Copy link. Nothing is sent until you pick a place. The place you pick then sees the link and the short message with it. This site loads nothing from those services and never learns whom you share with. If a link would show roughly where you vote, the page says so first. The Access page says more.

Can I get it as an app?

Yes. You can keep this site on your phone, tablet or computer, installed from the site itself: free, with no app store and no account, and it updates itself. Pages you have opened, and a ballot you kept, still work with no connection. Installing sends nothing to this site; on Android, your browser’s maker (Google, for Chrome) builds the app from the site’s public description of itself. Get the app shows how.

Why should I trust the ratings?

You do not have to take them on trust. Facts come from official records and link back to them. Ratings are judgments, kept apart and labelled. Each one shows its evidence grade, its reasons, its sources and the version of the rubric it was made under.

So far ten bills carry a full set of ratings. They were applied by hand, by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, as a preview, and each says so. Who backed a bill is worked out from the recorded votes by a published formula. The Method page has the whole rubric.
