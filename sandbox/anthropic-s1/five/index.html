Sandbox · Anthropic’s prospectus

In five minutes

Anthropic, the company that makes the Claude AI models, filed this document with the SEC on October 26, 2026, to sell shares to the public for the first time. It runs about 420 pages, counting artwork and Part II. Here it is in the same order, in about 1,200 words.

A sandbox page, not part of the public record this site keeps. The words are ours, not the filing’s; the figures are the filing’s own. Several numbers, such as the share price range, are still blank in this first version, as is usual; they arrive in amendments before any sale. This site is built with the help of Claude, made by Anthropic; Anthropic had no part in this page. Not investment advice.

The cover

What is for sale

Anthropic, PBC, a public benefit corporation formed in Delaware and based in San Francisco, plans to list Class A shares under the ticker ANT on Nasdaq’s Global Select Market. Some existing owners will sell shares too; Anthropic receives money only for its own. The price range and the number of shares are blank. Twenty-two firms are named as underwriters, led by Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, and the filing describes public offerings abroad too: in Australia, Canada, Japan, Switzerland, the United Kingdom and some countries of the European Economic Area.

The company’s own summary

Founded in 2021, Anthropic calls itself first a research lab, with a mission of developing advanced AI responsibly for humanity’s long-term benefit. It names five advantages: its mission, its people and culture, research that joins capability with safety, how it manages computing power, and its focus on businesses. It sells Claude directly, through apps such as Claude Code and Claude Cowork, and through Amazon’s, Google’s and Microsoft’s clouds. At September 30, 2026, more than 11,900 customers had paid it at least $100,000 over the past year, and about 250 had paid $10 million or more. Customers it had a year earlier were spending about nine times as much (net revenue retention of about 900 percent). It had raised $141.1 billion by June 30, 2026, and held $131.7 billion in cash and short-term investments. It has given 5 percent of its shares to its own Public Benefit Fund and may add up to 2 percent more each time the share price rises by half. Each co-founder has pledged 80 percent of their own shares to charity, a voluntary pledge that is not legally binding.

Pages 26 to 119

Risk factors

The longest part: 94 pages in six groups, from the business itself to owning the shares. Among them: it must keep building the most capable models and find enough computing power; it depends on its founders and key staff; competition is intense; its models could be misused, or act in harmful ways nobody directed, and its contracts and insurance may not cover the cost; a few customers, channels and cloud suppliers matter a great deal, and some suppliers are also investors and customers; its history is short; regulation is unsettled; and the share structure leaves Class A holders little say.

Forward-looking statements, and where outside figures come from

A standard warning that plans and predictions may not come true, and the sources of outside market figures, IDC and IQVIA among them, with how the company estimates the yearly value of knowledge work (its figure of more than $40 trillion for 2025 appears in the summary).

The money raised, and dividends

The amount is blank. The money is for general purposes, including computing and infrastructure and hiring, possibly acquisitions (no material one is agreed), and taxes due when employees’ stock units pay out. Anthropic has never paid a dividend, does not expect to, and its credit agreement limits them.

Tables of how the company is financed before and after the sale, and how far the price new buyers pay exceeds the company’s net tangible book value per share. Most figures wait for the price.

Management’s discussion of the numbers

Revenue was $41 million in 2023, $386 million in 2024 and $4.6 billion in 2025, then $16.3 billion in the first half of 2026 alone, against $1.2 billion a year earlier. Costs in that half came to $17.0 billion, the largest part computing and infrastructure ($8.9 billion), for an operating loss of $693 million; its own adjusted measure, which leaves out $2.2 billion of stock pay among other items, shows $1.6 billion of operating income. The half’s net loss of $80.7 billion comes mostly from paper changes in the value of convertible notes held by investors, Amazon among them, and related instruments, which rose with the company’s valuation ($72.4 billion). No customer was more than 10 percent of revenue in the first nine months of 2026. 2025 included a $1.5 billion charge to settle a lawsuit the filing calls the Bartz litigation. The company says Claude wrote over 90 percent of the contributions to its codebase in September 2026.

The business

What it does at length: its models, platform and applications, customer examples, research and safety work, computing, competition (OpenAI and Google among the model makers, plus open-weight models and application makers), and its people: about 5,300 employees at September 30, 2026.

Read it
Pages 242 to 253

How it is governed

The unusual part. As a public benefit corporation, its directors must weigh shareholders’ financial interests, the people its conduct affects, and its stated purpose. A Long-Term Benefit Trust, with five trustee seats (four filled today) for people meant to have no financial stake in the company, holds one Class T share and elects a majority of the board. A company owned by the seven co-founders will hold one Class F share carrying 50.1 percent of the votes on most other questions, including electing the other directors. Employees other than the founders will get Class E shares, which vote on a few matters only and carry no share of the profits; strategic partners hold non-voting Class N shares. The founders’ share ends if their company dissolves or they give it up, or 450 days after fewer than three founders have remained in that company for 90 days in a row; the employees’ Class E shares end with it. Because the trust picks a board majority, Anthropic qualifies as a controlled company under Nasdaq’s rules.

Management and pay

The executive officers are Dario Amodei, chief executive; Daniela Amodei, president and board chair; and Krishna Rao, chief financial officer. Once the offering closes, Reed Hastings, Christopher Liddell and Vas Narasimhan will be the trust’s directors, with a fourth trust seat vacant, and Yasmin Razavi will be elected by the shareholders. The pay section covers salaries, stock awards, severance and the new equity and share purchase plans, with tables for 2025.

Insiders and owners

Dealings with insiders and large holders, from past funding rounds to payments to the trust. The ownership table shows directors and officers together owning 496,360,810 Class A shares before the sale, counting options, stock units and shares voted by proxy; the trust holds the Class T share, and the founders’ company the Class F share with 50.1 percent of the votes. Most percentages wait for the share count.

The shares themselves

The legal terms of Classes A, N, T, F and E, registration rights (Google’s and Broadcom’s among them) and anti-takeover provisions. Most existing holders are locked up for up to 180 days, with releases in stages from the first day of trading; the seven co-founders and some others for up to 365 days. Then how U.S. tax applies to buyers abroad: dividends, sales and withholding.

The underwriters’ terms: Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley act for all 22 firms; their fees, their option to buy extra shares, lock-ups, and selling rules for 25 places outside the United States.

Wilson Sonsini Goodrich & Rosati acts for the company, Freshfields for the underwriters, and the trust had advisers of its own. In October 2025 the company replaced its auditor, Grant Thornton, with Ernst & Young, and reports no disagreements; Grant Thornton audited 2023 and 2024, Ernst & Young 2025. Plain answers

Who pays for this site?

Nobody pays to appear on it. The site runs no advertising of its own and has no donors. It is published on GitHub's free public hosting.

Does this site track me?

This site counts nothing about its readers: no page sends a visit, a click or a place anywhere, except as described below. There is no account and no sign-in. Your reading settings and your companion are kept in this browser, on this device, and are never sent anywhere.

Where a page offers "Use my location", your place is worked out on your device and is not sent anywhere. Every page takes its type from this site. Three things come from other servers. The opening picture of Plain Congress shows the weather at the Capitol in Washington, from Open-Meteo; that request carries the Capitol's place, never yours. If you switch on street maps on a ballot or Forecasts page, OpenStreetMap's servers see which map squares are asked for, and the page says so. And if you type your address on a ballot or Forecasts page and press Look up, your browser sends it straight to the U.S. Census Bureau's address finder, which sends back where it is; this site never sees the address or keeps it.

What happens when I share a page?

A Share button sends a link. On a phone it opens your phone’s own share sheet; on a computer, a short list of places, such as e-mail, Facebook or LinkedIn, or Copy link. Nothing is sent until you pick a place. The place you pick then sees the link and the short message with it. This site loads nothing from those services and never learns whom you share with. If a link would show roughly where you vote, the page says so first. The Access page says more.

Can I get it as an app?

Yes. You can keep this site on your phone, tablet or computer, installed from the site itself: free, with no app store and no account, and it updates itself. Pages you have opened, and a ballot you kept, still work with no connection. Installing sends nothing to this site; on Android, your browser’s maker (Google, for Chrome) builds the app from the site’s public description of itself. Get the app shows how.

Why should I trust the ratings?

You do not have to take them on trust. Facts come from official records and link back to them. Ratings are judgments, kept apart and labelled. Each one shows its evidence grade, its reasons, its sources and the version of the rubric it was made under.

So far ten bills carry a full set of ratings. They were applied by hand, by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, as a preview, and each says so. Who backed a bill is worked out from the recorded votes by a published formula. The Method page has the whole rubric.
