Sandbox · a walkthrough

Phone alerts, with the site closed

Follow one alert from Congress to a reader’s pocket, one step at a time. Then see what it would take to build.

A sandbox page: an experiment on the draft site. It is not part of the public record this site keeps, it is not on the live site, and search engines are asked not to list it.

Where this stands

  • Nothing here is built, and nothing has been bought. This page explains a plan John asked to keep for later.
  • Today: Notify me shows Congress alerts on your device only while the site, or its app, is open.
  • The plan: alerts that reach a phone with the site closed. That takes one small new service. The steps below show why.
How deep should the words go?

The journey of one alert

    1. 1 A reader says yes

      A reader opens a bill and taps Notify me. The phone asks: allow notifications? The reader taps Allow. The phone asks only after a tap. No one can be signed up without saying yes.

      The reader presses Notify me on a bill, and the browser shows its own question: allow notifications from this site? The site asks only after a press, never when a page opens. A No stays a No until the reader changes it in the browser’s own settings. Without an Allow, nothing can ever be sent to that device.

      A user gesture calls Notification.requestPermission(). On "granted" the page calls registration.pushManager.subscribe({userVisibleOnly: true, applicationServerKey}) on the site’s existing service worker registration (Push API, W3C). applicationServerKey is the public half of the site’s VAPID (RFC 8292). On iPhone and iPad the Push API exists only inside a site added to the Home Screen, iOS and iPadOS 16.4 or later.

    2. 2 The phone gets a private delivery address

      Every browser has a kind of post office behind it. It is called a . Apple runs one for iPhones. Google runs one for Android and Chrome. Mozilla runs one for Firefox. The post office gives the phone a private delivery address. It is a long web address made of random letters. It holds no name, no e-mail and no phone number. Two keys come with it. They are used later to lock messages.

      The browser asks its maker’s for a subscription. Back comes an : a long web address nobody could guess, standing for this browser, on this device, for this site only. Two keys made on the phone come with it. One is public: anyone may use it to lock a message for this phone. The other is a shared secret that shows a message was meant for it. The phone keeps a third, private key that never leaves it. No name, e-mail or phone number is involved, and the push services charge a site nothing.

      subscribe() resolves to a PushSubscription: the , a capability URL at the browser’s push service (fcm.googleapis.com for Chrome, web.push.apple.com for Safari, updates.push.services.mozilla.com for Firefox; Edge uses Microsoft’s); getKey("p256dh"), the client’s ECDH P-256 public key, 65 bytes uncompressed; and getKey("auth"), a 16-byte authentication secret. toJSON() gives all three ready to send. The subscription is bound to the applicationServerKey it was made with: no other key can send to it.

    3. 3 The address goes to the sign-up service

      The page sends the address to a small sign-up service. The service keeps it. If the reader chose “only bills I follow”, the list of those bills goes too. Why is this needed? Today the site is like a shelf of pages. It can hand pages out. It cannot take anything in, and it cannot remember anything. So one small helper on the internet has to keep the sign-ups. That helper is the missing piece.

      The page sends the and its two keys to the sign-up service, which keeps them as one line in a list. If the reader chose “only bills I follow” or “my members”, those choices go too, because the choosing has to happen where the sending happens (step 7 says why). The site’s pages are served by GitHub Pages, which only hands out files. It runs no code of ours and keeps nothing a reader sends. That is why this one small service is the piece that does not exist yet.

      A fetch() POST of subscription.toJSON() plus an optional filter (measure keys, member ids) to one HTTPS address, with CORS limited to the site’s origin. Storage is one table: endpoint (unique), p256dh, auth, filter, created. No account, no cookie. Any small serverless function with a key-value or SQL store can do it; GitHub Pages cannot, being static hosting with no request handlers and no writable storage. The service should limit how fast sign-ups arrive, accept only endpoints whose host is a known push service, and keep no request logs beyond what its host keeps against abuse.

    4. 4 Congress does something

      Every 90 minutes, the site checks the official record of Congress. This time there is news. A bill passed the House.

      The site already refreshes its Congress pages every 90 minutes from the official record. While a chamber is voting, it also looks for new roll calls every 15 minutes. Each look compares the record with what was there last time and writes one alert for each new event. This time: a bill passed the House. Today that alert reaches only readers who have the site open.

      The existing job compares the record with its last-seen state and emits one alert object per event (kind, measure, chamber, tally, next step), each with a stable id. Those objects are already published as a small JSON file that open pages poll. For push, the same object is the payload: a few hundred bytes, far under the 4,096 bytes every push service must accept (RFC 8030, section 7.2).

    5. 5 A locked message for each sign-up

      Now a message is made for each sign-up. It is locked, so only that one phone can open it. Not even the post office can read it. This is called . The message is also signed with the site’s own key. The signature tells the post office the message really comes from this site.

      For each kept sign-up that wants this alert, the sender locks the message with that phone’s public key (), so the push service carries it without being able to read it. The sender also signs a short pass with the private half of the site’s . The push service checks the pass against the public half, which the reader’s browser was given in step 1. So nobody else can send to this site’s readers. The private half is the one real secret in the whole design, and it is never put on the website.

      Per subscription: ECDH between a fresh ephemeral P-256 key and p256dh, HKDF salted with the auth secret, then AES-128-GCM, sent with Content-Encoding: aes128gcm (RFC 8291, over RFC 8188). The request carries Authorization: vapid t=<JWT>, k=<public key>; the JWT is signed ES256, with aud the push service’s origin, exp no more than 24 hours ahead and sub a contact address (RFC 8292). Well-tested open-source libraries do all of this, and it should not be written by hand.

    6. 6 Handed to the post office

      The locked message goes to the post office. The post office holds it until the phone is online. Then it delivers it. The site can be closed. The browser can be closed too. Sending costs nothing.

      The sender hands each locked message to the named in its address, with a note saying how long to keep trying. For a vote, a few hours is plenty. The push service keeps one quiet line open to every phone that uses it, which is how a message arrives with the site and the browser both closed. Apple, Google and Mozilla charge a site nothing for this.

      An HTTP POST to the endpoint (Web Push protocol, RFC 8030) with TTL, the seconds the service may hold the message (0 means deliver now or drop); optionally Urgency (very-low to high) and Topic (a newer message with the same topic replaces an older one not yet delivered). 201 Created means accepted, not delivered. 429 means slow down: honour Retry-After. Each send is its own request, so many go out side by side.

    7. 7 The phone shows it

      On the phone, a small helper that belongs to the site wakes up. It is called a . It unlocks the message and shows the notification. A tap opens the bill. The helper is already part of the site. It is what lets the site work as an app.

      The browser wakes the site’s , the same background helper that already lets the site be installed and read offline. It unlocks the message and must show a notification. Browsers do not allow silent pushes. That is why a message cannot be sent to every phone and then hidden on the ones that do not follow that bill: the choosing has to be done before sending. A tap opens the bill’s page. On iPhone and iPad this works only when the site has been added to the Home Screen (iOS or iPadOS 16.4 or later), which is what Get the app does. On Android it works from Chrome or from the installed app.

      The browser decrypts and fires a push event in the service worker, which calls event.waitUntil(registration.showNotification(title, {body, tag, data: {url}})). With userVisibleOnly: true every push must show a notification: Chrome shows a generic one if the worker does not, and Safari can end the subscription. The worker’s existing notificationclick handler focuses or opens data.url. It should also answer pushsubscriptionchange by subscribing again and sending the new endpoint.

    8. 8 Housekeeping

      A reader can stop at any time. They tap Unsubscribe, and the sign-up is deleted. What if someone turns notifications off in the phone’s settings instead? Then the post office answers “gone”, and the sign-up is removed too.

      Unsubscribe does two things: the browser gives up its address, and the page tells the sign-up service to delete that line. If a reader instead switches notifications off in the phone’s settings, or removes the app, the next send gets the answer “gone” from the push service, and the sender deletes the line. Nothing is kept about someone who has left.

      subscription.unsubscribe(), then a DELETE to the sign-up service keyed by endpoint. On a send, HTTP 404 or 410 from the push service means the subscription has expired or is gone: delete the row and never retry it. Endpoints can also be replaced by the browser (pushsubscriptionchange), and the old rows are pruned the same way. No record of deleted rows is kept.

    How we would build it, step by step

    Seven jobs, in order. Tick them off as you read; the ticks are kept on this device only. The times are estimates, not promises.

    1. Who
      John decides. Claude lays the choices side by side. Opening an account is John’s own step.
      About how long
      An evening to compare; a quarter of an hour to open an account.
      Could it cost money?
      Possibly. Some services have a free tier with limits, and some charge by use. Prices and limits change, so each one’s own price page has to be read before choosing. Nothing has been chosen. John is asked before anything costs money.
      What could go wrong
      A free tier’s limit is passed on a busy night. A service closes or changes its terms. So the list of sign-ups must be something we can take out and move.
    2. Who
      Claude makes it on John’s computer. John keeps the private half.
      About how long
      Minutes.
      Could it cost money?
      No.
      What could go wrong
      The private half is lost or leaks. Every sign-up is tied to the pair, so a new pair means every reader signs up again. The private half is kept off the website and out of anything public; only the public half goes on the pages.
    3. Who
      Claude.
      About how long
      A day or two, with testing.
      Could it cost money?
      No.
      What could go wrong
      An iPhone reader has not added the site to the Home Screen, so the page must say how. A reader said No once, and the browser will not ask again, so the page must say where to change it. Today’s alerts, with the site open, must keep working exactly as they do.
    4. Who
      Claude.
      About how long
      About a day.
      Could it cost money?
      The push services are free. Each send that passes through the sign-up service counts toward whatever limits it has (job 1).
      What could go wrong
      The same alert goes out twice, so each one is marked once sent. John’s computer is off, so nothing is sent until it is back, as with today’s updates; the other way is to let the service do the sending itself, which is a choice to make in job 1. A reader gets it twice, once by push and once from an open page, so both carry the same tag and the phone shows one.
    5. Who
      John. Claude drafts; a draft is below.
      About how long
      An hour.
      Could it cost money?
      No.
      What could go wrong
      The promise and the site disagree. The words “nothing leaves your device” appear in several places, and every one has to change on the same day the feature goes out.
    6. Who
      John and Claude, on the draft site first.
      About how long
      An afternoon.
      Could it cost money?
      No.
      What could go wrong
      A battery saver holds notifications back for a while. Nobody has tried it on an iPhone, so one has to be borrowed. An alert opens the wrong page, or the draft’s alert opens the live site.
    7. Who
      John says when. The draft site first, then the live one.
      About how long
      A week on the draft, then one switch.
      Could it cost money?
      Only if sign-ups outgrow the service’s limits (job 1), which would be seen coming.
      What could go wrong
      Too many alerts, and readers switch them all off. So the starting choice would be the big moments only, with “everything” for those who ask.

    What changes for readers’ privacy

    Today

    • Nothing about a reader leaves their device.
    • Notify me, follows and reminders are kept on the device only.
    • Alerts show only while the site or its app is open.

    With phone alerts

    • For readers who sign up, and only them: a delivery address and its two keys are kept on the sign-up service.
    • If they chose “only bills I follow” or “my members”, that list is kept beside it.
    • Unsubscribe deletes all of it. So does switching notifications off.
    • Still never kept: a name, an e-mail address, a phone number, a location.
    • Everyone who does not sign up: nothing changes.

    Worth knowing: a list of followed bills, kept beside an address, says something about what that reader cares about, even with no name on it. A reader who would rather not share it can choose the big moments instead, which sends no list.

    A draft: the wording is John’s call

    “If you turn on phone alerts, we keep one thing: the delivery address your browser gave us, so that an alert can reach you with the site closed. It has no name, e-mail address or phone number in it. If you asked for alerts only about bills or members you follow, we keep that list with it. Unsubscribe, and both are deleted. If you never turn on phone alerts, nothing about you leaves your device.”

    The words, explained

    Words with a dotted line in the steps open these notes where you are reading.

    Push service
    The post office behind a browser. Apple runs one for Safari, Google for Chrome, Mozilla for Firefox. It keeps one quiet line open to each phone and passes messages along it. It cannot read them. A site pays nothing to use it.
    Endpoint
    The private delivery address a push service gives one browser, on one device, for one site. A long web address of random letters. It says nothing about who the reader is.
    Service worker
    A small helper a site leaves in the browser. It can wake for a moment when a message comes, even with the site closed. This site already has one: it is what makes the site work as an app and open pages offline.
    Encryption
    Locking a message so that only the one who holds the right key can read it. Here each alert is locked for one phone, so the post office carries it without seeing inside.
    Key pair
    Two matching keys. The public one can be handed to anyone. The private one is kept secret. What one locks or signs, the other opens or checks. The site would have one pair, to prove an alert is really from the site.

    Plain answers

    Who pays for this site?

    Nobody pays to appear on it. The site runs no advertising of its own and has no donors. It is published on GitHub's free public hosting.

    Does this site track me?

    This site counts nothing about its readers: no page sends a visit, a click or a place anywhere, except as described below. There is no account and no sign-in. Your reading settings and your companion are kept in this browser, on this device, and are never sent anywhere.

    Where a page offers "Use my location", your place is worked out on your device and is not sent anywhere. Every page takes its type from this site. Three things come from other servers. The opening picture of Plain Congress shows the weather at the Capitol in Washington, from Open-Meteo; that request carries the Capitol's place, never yours. If you switch on street maps on a ballot or Forecasts page, OpenStreetMap's servers see which map squares are asked for, and the page says so. And if you type your address on a ballot or Forecasts page and press Look up, your browser sends it straight to the U.S. Census Bureau's address finder, which sends back where it is; this site never sees the address or keeps it.

    What happens when I share a page?

    A Share button sends a link. On a phone it opens your phone’s own share sheet; on a computer, a short list of places, such as e-mail, Facebook or LinkedIn, or Copy link. Nothing is sent until you pick a place. The place you pick then sees the link and the short message with it. This site loads nothing from those services and never learns whom you share with. If a link would show roughly where you vote, the page says so first. The Access page says more.

    Can I get it as an app?

    Yes. You can keep this site on your phone, tablet or computer, installed from the site itself: free, with no app store and no account, and it updates itself. Pages you have opened, and a ballot you kept, still work with no connection. Installing sends nothing to this site; on Android, your browser’s maker (Google, for Chrome) builds the app from the site’s public description of itself. Get the app shows how.

    Why should I trust the ratings?

    You do not have to take them on trust. Facts come from official records and link back to them. Ratings are judgments, kept apart and labelled. Each one shows its evidence grade, its reasons, its sources and the version of the rubric it was made under.

    So far ten bills carry a full set of ratings. They were applied by hand, by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, as a preview, and each says so. Who backed a bill is worked out from the recorded votes by a published formula. The Method page has the whole rubric.
