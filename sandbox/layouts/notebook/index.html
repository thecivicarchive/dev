    How to use this notebook

    The short of it

    The main pages keep to what most readers want. Everything that supports them, the sources, the working, the full tables and what a figure cannot tell you, lives here, one page per topic.

    Every notebook link on a main page looks like this: a link with a notebook mark. It opens the exact page here, and a Back to button takes you to where you were.

    The rail on the left (the Contents button on a phone) starts with the site’s spaces and stacks the pages under each, so you can also browse it like a book. Pages marked outline show where a page would go; they are not written for this example.

    You can pin a page and keep a margin note on any page. Both stay on this device only; nothing is sent.

    Analysis
    The short of it

    The model’s last run before Election Day gave Roy Cooper more than 99 in 100 chance, likely 49.1% to 56.4% of the vote. The count has Roy Cooper at 58.8%: above that range.

    The last run, November 1, 2026: each candidate’s likely share, and the result
    CandidateChanceMiddle80 in 10095 in 100The count
    Roy Coopermore than 99 in 10053.1%49.1% to 56.4%46.9% to 57.9%58.8%
    Michael Whatleyless than 1 in 10042.6%39.2% to 45.8%37.0% to 47.4%39.0%

    Roy Cooper’s share in the count, 58.8%, is outside the 95-in-100 range the model gave (46.9% to 57.9%). The model ran 2,000 simulated elections; a result outside its range is a miss, and it is counted in the track record, not explained away.

    In the model’s own words: In 10 other states, U.S. Senate polls by members of AAPOR's Transparency Initiative have Democrats running 3.2 points ahead of Kamala Harris's 2024 share of the two-party vote. Applied to North Carolina's 2024 presidential result (48.4% Democratic of the two-party vote) and moved a little by North Carolina's own member polls, a generic 2026 contest in North Carolina stands near 53.4% Democratic, give or take about 3.6 points.

    Votes left blank and split results

    Analysis
    The short of it

    How many fewer votes this race had than the top race in each county, and the counties that chose different parties for different races. Worked out on Election Night’s page from the same county figures.

    In North Carolina this year the U.S. Senate race is itself the race with the most votes, so “votes left blank” is measured for the races below it, against this one.

    A gap between two races in a county can come from voters who chose different parties, from voters who skipped a race, and from third-party and write-in candidates. The page never says that anyone split a ticket.

    What it cannot tell you

    • County totals cannot show any one ballot.
    • A line drawn through places says nothing about how any voter voted.

    Election Night › North Carolina › U.S. Senate

    When the count becomes final

    How it works
    The short of it

    Election night figures are a first count. The result is final when the state certifies it.

    1. The first count. Counties report as they count; the state posts the running totals. Provisional and some mailed ballots are counted after Election Day.
    2. The canvass. Each county board checks its count, adds the ballots that could not be counted on the night and approves its results.
    3. Certification. The State Board of Elections certifies the statewide result. This site marks a figure “as certified” only then.
    4. Recounts and challenges. A close race can be recounted under state law, and candidates can go to court. A concession is a custom, not a legal step: it ends no count.

    What it cannot tell you

    • It gives no dates: each state sets its own, and this example does not repeat them.

    Forecasts

    How the forecast works

    Analysis
    The short of it

    A computer model’s estimate from past election results and polls by members of AAPOR’s Transparency Initiative.

    For a statewide race it starts from how the state voted for President in 2024, moves it by members’ polls of other states’ Senate races and by members’ polls of this race, and sizes its own uncertainty on the 2022 and 2024 races. It then runs thousands of simulated elections; the ranges hold 80 and 95 in every 100 of them.

    Every run is stored with what went into it, so it can be made again exactly, and the forecast never says 0 or 100 percent.

    Forecasts

    The forecast’s track record

    Analysis
    The short of it

    Tested on the U.S. Senate races of 2022 and 2024 (64 races): the favourite won 62; the actual Democratic share fell inside the 80-in-100 range in 58 and inside the 95-in-100 range in 63; vote shares were off by 3.9 points on average.

    A track record is how the same model would have done on elections already decided. It is the best guide to how far to trust a range, and it is shown wherever a forecast is.

    How we work
    The short of it

    Every figure carries a label saying what kind of fact it is.

    Official record
    Copied from a government’s own record, unchanged.
    Official count
    A government’s count of votes. Until it is certified it is also marked:
    As reported, not final
    The latest figures the state has posted; they can still change.

      Plain answers

      Who pays for this site?

      Nobody pays to appear on it. The site runs no advertising of its own and has no donors. It is published on GitHub's free public hosting.

      Does this site track me?

      This site counts nothing about its readers: no page sends a visit, a click or a place anywhere, except as described below. There is no account and no sign-in. Your reading settings and your companion are kept in this browser, on this device, and are never sent anywhere.

      Where a page offers "Use my location", your place is worked out on your device and is not sent anywhere. Every page takes its type from this site. Three things come from other servers. The opening picture of Plain Congress shows the weather at the Capitol in Washington, from Open-Meteo; that request carries the Capitol's place, never yours. If you switch on street maps on a ballot or Forecasts page, OpenStreetMap's servers see which map squares are asked for, and the page says so. And if you type your address on a ballot or Forecasts page and press Look up, your browser sends it straight to the U.S. Census Bureau's address finder, which sends back where it is; this site never sees the address or keeps it.

      What happens when I share a page?

      A Share button sends a link. On a phone it opens your phone’s own share sheet; on a computer, a short list of places, such as e-mail, Facebook or LinkedIn, or Copy link. Nothing is sent until you pick a place. The place you pick then sees the link and the short message with it. This site loads nothing from those services and never learns whom you share with. If a link would show roughly where you vote, the page says so first. The Access page says more.

      Can I get it as an app?

      Yes. You can keep this site on your phone, tablet or computer, installed from the site itself: free, with no app store and no account, and it updates itself. Pages you have opened, and a ballot you kept, still work with no connection. Installing sends nothing to this site; on Android, your browser’s maker (Google, for Chrome) builds the app from the site’s public description of itself. Get the app shows how.

      Why should I trust the ratings?

      You do not have to take them on trust. Facts come from official records and link back to them. Ratings are judgments, kept apart and labelled. Each one shows its evidence grade, its reasons, its sources and the version of the rubric it was made under.

      So far ten bills carry a full set of ratings. They were applied by hand, by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, as a preview, and each says so. Who backed a bill is worked out from the recorded votes by a published formula. The Method page has the whole rubric.
